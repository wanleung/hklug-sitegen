Title: Google Flags a Tipping Point: AI That Finds and Exploits Bugs Without a Human in the Loop

<h3>Google Flags a Tipping Point: AI That Finds and Exploits Bugs Without a Human in the Loop</h3>
<p>Fully automated AI-driven attacks are closer than the security industry generally assumes, according to Google — with the company warning that agentic workflows are already being used to compress the gap between finding a flaw and abusing it.</p>
<p>According to BleepingComputer's report on Google's warning, threat actors are leaning on agentic workflows powered by AI-based offensive frameworks to automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation, pushing attacks to machine speed. Google frames the milestone as imminent rather than a distant one.</p>
<h4>Not a chatbot — a workflow</h4>
<p>The important shift is structural, not conversational. What Google and Mandiant describe is not an AI assistant answering a phisher's questions; it is a pipeline in which reconnaissance, vulnerability identification, exploit development and delivery are chained together as automated steps.</p>
<p>The practical consequence is that the human role in the attack chain shrinks to setting an objective and approving the outcome. Everything between those two points — the tedious, latency-heavy middle that has historically given defenders room to breathe — can run without a person in the loop.</p>
<p>That matters because human latency has quietly been a defensive asset. Manual recon, manual triage, manual exploit development stretched intrusions over days and weeks, and those windows are what detection and incident-response processes are built around. If the bottleneck disappears, detection windows compress from days to minutes, and runbooks tuned for slow-burn intrusions lose their assumptions.</p>
<h4>What defenders should change first</h4>
<p>Google's claim here is scoped to the automation itself: the company is describing what attackers are building, not publishing a defensive playbook. The two adjustments below are our reading of what logically follows — defensive steps consistent with Google's framing of machine-speed attacks.</p>
<p><strong>Sequence-based detection:</strong> Instead of hunting for isolated indicators, defenders should model the order in which automated tools typically operate — asset enumeration followed by scanning, scanning followed by exploit attempts, exploitation followed by discovery and exfiltration. Alerts that individually look benign can become meaningful when they appear in that sequence, within a compressed timeframe.</p>
<p><strong>Pre-emptive hardening:</strong> Reactive triage assumes time to assess. When the assessment window collapses, the leverage moves earlier: patching internet-facing services before they are enumerated, closing unnecessary exposure, and reducing the attack surface that an automated workflow can find in the first place. The question shifts from "what is this alert telling us?" to "what could an agent reach in five minutes?"</p>
<h4>Why this lands with lean teams</h4>
<p>The dynamics Google describes apply to security teams everywhere, including those running lean functions against large internet-facing estates. Organisations that rely on manual review as a de facto control are the most exposed to a workflow that removes manual review from the attacker's side of the equation.</p>
<p>Google's stated position also underlines a familiar asymmetry in defensive automation: attackers need only find one reachable flaw, while defenders have to close the paths an agent can traverse before it traverses them.</p>
<h4>The detection-engineer checklist</h4>
<p>For detection engineering and incident response, the payoff is concrete: correlation rules and alert-queue SLAs must be re-tested against a minutes-scale timeline. A correlation rule that assumes a half-day gap between enumeration and exploitation, or an SLA that promises triage within four hours, quietly encodes a human-speed attack that Google says is about to disappear. Escalation steps that implicitly wait for a person to notice are now the weakest link.</p>
<p>The actionable takeaway is unambiguous — treat the human in the loop as no longer guaranteed on either side of the fight, and rebuild detection and response processes accordingly.</p>

<h3>Google 指出臨界點：AI 無需人手參與即可尋找並利用漏洞</h3>
<p>Google 表示，全自動化、由 AI 驅動的攻擊比安全業界普遍認為的更為迫近，並警告 agentic workflows 已經被用來縮短從發現漏洞到濫用漏洞之間的時間差。</p>
<p>據 BleepingComputer 就 Google 警告所作的報道，威脅者正倚賴以 AI 攻擊框架驅動的 agentic workflows，將漏洞發現及利用過程自動化，令攻擊速度提升至機器速度。Google 將此里程碑形容為即將到來，而非遙不可及。</p>
<h4>不是聊天機械人——而是工作流程</h4>
<p>重要的轉變在於架構層面，而非對話形式。Google 與 Mandiant 描述的並非一個 AI 助手回答釣魚者的提問；而是一條將偵察、漏洞識別、exploit 開發及投放串連為自動化步驟的 pipeline。</p>
<p>實際後果是，人類在攻擊鏈中的角色縮減至設定目標及批准結果。介乎這兩點之間的一切——歷來令防守方得以喘息、冗長而延遲沉重的中間環節——均可在沒有人手參與的情況下運行。</p>
<p>這一點至關重要，因為人類的延遲一直以來都是防守方的隱性資產。人工偵察、人工分類、人工 exploit 開發令入侵過程延續數天甚至數星期，而偵測及事故應對流程正是圍繞這些時間窗口而建立。一旦瓶頸消失，偵測窗口將由數天壓縮至數分鐘，針對緩慢持續入侵而調校的 runbooks 亦將失去其前提假設。</p>
<h4>防守方應優先改變甚麼</h4>
<p>Google 在此的說法僅限於自動化本身：該公司描述的是攻擊者正在構建的東西，並非發布防守 playbook。以下兩項調整是我們按邏輯推論得出的解讀——與 Google 對機器速度攻擊的定調一致的防守步驟。</p>
<p><strong>基於序列的偵測：</strong> 防守方不應只搜尋孤立的 indicators，而應建立模型，掌握自動化工具通常運作的次序——資產枚舉之後是掃描，掃描之後是 exploit 嘗試，利用成功之後是發現及資料外洩。個別看似良性的警報，若在壓縮的時間範圍內依此次序出現，便可轉化為有意義的訊號。</p>
<p><strong>先發制人的加固：</strong> 被動式分類的前提是有時間進行評估。當評估窗口崩塌時，著力點須前移：在對外服務被枚舉之前先行 patch、關閉不必要的暴露面，以及盡量減少自動化 workflow 一開始便能發現的 attack surface。問題會由「這個警報在告訴我們甚麼？」轉變為「一個 agent 五分鐘內可以觸及甚麼？」</p>
<h4>為何精簡團隊尤其受影響</h4>
<p>Google 所描述的動態適用於各地的防守團隊，包括以精簡人力應對大規模對外資產的團隊。依賴人工審核作為事實上的控制手段的機構，在攻擊方環節不再需要人工審核的工作流程面前，暴露程度最高。</p>
<p>Google 公開表明的立場亦突顯防守自動化中一項常見的不對稱性：攻擊者只需找到一個可觸及的漏洞，而防守方則須在 agent 到達之前封堵其所有可通行的路徑。</p>
<h4>偵測工程師清單</h4>
<p>對偵測工程及事故應對而言，實際影響十分明確：correlation rules 及 alert queue SLA 必須按分鐘級別的時間線重新測試。一條假設枚舉與利用之間相隔半日的 correlation rule，或一個承諾四小時內完成分類的 SLA，其實隱含編碼了 Google 所稱即將消失的人手速度攻擊。隱含等待人手察覺的升級處理步驟，如今已是最薄弱的環節。</p>
<p>可立即採取的結論十分明確——不要假設攻防任何一方仍會有人手參與，並據此重建偵測及應對流程。</p>
