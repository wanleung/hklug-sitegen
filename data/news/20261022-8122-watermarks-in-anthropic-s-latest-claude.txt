<h3>Watermarks in Anthropic's Latest Claude Output Push AI Provenance onto the Compliance Agenda</h3>
<p>Organisations deploying Anthropic's Claude models may need to treat invisible text watermarks as an operational governance variable — affecting disclosure practices, vendor contracts and content-retention policies — rather than as a passing technical curiosity. That is the framing emerging from a practitioner's account published on O'Reilly Radar, which describes encountering watermarked output from the latest Claude models during a live training session and works through what the finding implies for everyday users.</p>
<p>The piece is written as a working practitioner's observation rather than a technical disclosure from Anthropic. What it does not contain matters for anyone tempted to build policy on it: the article does not document the watermarking mechanism, clarify which Claude models carry it, or indicate how long the signal is expected to last. That gap should temper any internal summary — the finding reached practitioners' screens, but the engineering detail behind it is not in front of us.</p>
<p>For background: text watermarking of the kind generally discussed in the literature works by subtly biasing token selection during generation according to a pseudorandom pattern keyed to a secret. The statistical footprint of that pattern can then be tested in a candidate passage without access to the original prompt or session. This is offered here as general context on how such systems are typically built — not as a description of Anthropic's specific implementation.</p>
<p>The crux for policy is durability. A signal that survives copy-paste, republication and downstream editing can, in principle, be read by parties the author never contemplated — regulators, counterparties, insurers or acquirers during due diligence. Provenance that persists past the point of publication is therefore a contracting and disclosure question, not merely a detection question.</p>
<p>Three steps are worth considering for teams reviewing their AI usage policies:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Map disclosure obligations.</strong> Determine where AI-assisted text enters external-facing communications, contracts or regulated filings, and whether internal policy or counterparty terms require identification of such content.</li>
<li><strong>Add watermarking to vendor diligence.</strong> When evaluating AI providers, ask directly whether outputs are watermarked, what the signal's expected lifetime is, and whether the vendor documents its behaviour.</li>
<li><strong>Stress-test content workflows.</strong> Verify that any downstream tooling — translation, summarisation, plagiarism or detection checks — behaves predictably when presented with watermarked text.</li>
</ol>
<p>Several caveats should survive any internal summary of this story. Watermarks can degrade under translation, paraphrasing or heavy editing, so their absence proves nothing about a text's origin. Detection tests can also produce false positives on ordinary human writing. There is no cross-vendor standard: a watermark applied by one provider will not necessarily be detectable by another vendor's tooling. And because implementation details matter, organisations should verify current behaviour with Anthropic directly rather than relying on third-party accounts.</p>
<p>For Hong Kong organisations, the practical relevance is generic rather than regulatory at this stage: firms that use generative AI for customer communications, disclosure documents or marketing copy may find that provenance expectations are set by contract terms and counterparties as much as by statute. Whether data-handling obligations under the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance are engaged will depend on the specific facts, and readers should confirm the position with counsel rather than assume a general rule.</p>
<p>Ultimately, the story matters to the wider IT community because it marks a shift in how generated text can be treated. Detection tools ask whether content looks machine-made; watermarks, where durable, offer an affirmative provenance signal that third parties may rely on. Content policy, vendor contracts and records management are the unglamorous places that question will actually be settled.</p>

<h3>Anthropic 最新 Claude 輸出加入水印，將 AI 內容溯源推上合規議程</h3>
<p>部署 Anthropic Claude 模型的機構，或需將無形文字水印視為一項營運治理變數——它會影響披露慣例、供應商合約及內容保留政策——而非一時的技術新奇玩意。這是 O'Reilly Radar 上一篇實務從業人員文章所提出的框架，作者講述在一場實戰培訓課程中遇到最新 Claude 模型的帶水印輸出，並逐步分析這項發現對一般用戶的意涵。</p>
<p>該文以實務從業人員的觀察形式寫成，而非 Anthropic 的技術披露。文中「沒有」提及的事項，對任何想憑此建立政策的人同樣重要：文章沒有記錄水印機制、沒有說明哪些 Claude 型號載有水印，亦沒有指出該信號預期可維持多久。這個缺口應令任何內部摘要有所保留——該發現確實出現於從業人員的螢幕上，但其背後的工程細節並未擺在我們面前。</p>
<p>背景資料：文獻中一般討論的文字水印技術，原理是在生成過程中，按一個以秘密金鑰編碼的 pseudorandom 模式，微妙地偏移 token 選擇。該模式的統計足跡隨後可在一段候選文字中測試出來，無需取得原始 prompt 或會話。這裡提供的是關於此類系統一般如何建構的通用背景——並非對 Anthropic 具體實現的描述。</p>
<p>對政策而言，關鍵在於持久性。一個能經受複製貼上、轉載及後續編輯的信號，原則上可被作者從未設想的各方讀出——包括監管機構、交易對手、保險公司，或盡職調查中的收購方。因此，發布後仍然存在的溯源問題，是合約與披露問題，而不單純是偵測問題。</p>
<p>檢視 AI 使用政策的團隊，值得考慮三個步驟：</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>梳理披露責任。</strong> 確定 AI 輔助文本在哪些環節進入對外通訊、合約或受規管的申報文件，以及內部政策或交易對手條款是否要求標示此類內容。</li>
<li><strong>將水印納入供應商盡職審查。</strong> 評估 AI 供應商時，直接詢問其輸出是否帶有水印、信號的預期壽命為何，以及供應商是否就其行為提供文件說明。</li>
<li><strong>壓力測試內容工作流程。</strong> 確認任何下游工具——翻譯、摘要、抄襲或偵測檢查——在處理帶水印文本時行為可預期。</li>
</ol>
<p>任何內部摘要都應保留若干注意事項。水印在翻譯、改寫或大量編輯下可能退化，因此沒有水印並不能證明文本的來源。偵測測試亦可能對普通人類撰寫的文字產生 false positive。目前並無跨供應商標準：一家供應商施加的水印，未必能被另一家供應商的工具偵測到。而且由於實現細節影響重大，機構應直接向 Anthropic 確認目前行為，而非依賴第三方說法。</p>
<p>對香港機構而言，現階段的實際關聯較屬通用性質而非監管性質：使用生成式 AI 處理客戶通訊、披露文件或市場推廣文案的企業，或會發現溯源期望既由合約條款及交易對手設定，亦不亞於由法例設定。《個人資料（私隱）條例》下的資料處理責任是否適用，須視乎具體事實而定，讀者應向法律顧問確認立場，而非假設存在一般規則。</p>
<p>歸根究底，這則事件對更廣泛的 IT 社群重要，因為它標誌著生成文本處理方式的轉變。偵測工具問的是內容是否像機器生成；而持久可靠的水印則提供一個肯定的溯源信號，可供第三方依賴。內容政策、供應商合約及紀錄管理，正是這個問題最終落地之處，雖然毫不光鮮。</p>
