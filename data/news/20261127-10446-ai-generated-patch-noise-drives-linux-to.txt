Title: AI-Generated Patch Noise Drives Linux to Drop 1980s VME Bus Code
Content:
<p>```</p>
<p>The Linux kernel is preparing to strip out the last of its VersaModular Eurocard (VME) bus support, and the stated reason says more about the present of kernel development than about the hardware of the 1980s: AI and large language models are generating an increasing volume of low-value patches against old, unused code.</p>
<p>As Phoronix reports, the VME bus drivers represent the latest in a line of vintage subsystems being retired from the source tree. Older rounds of cleanup have already removed obsolete ISA and PCMCIA drivers, ham radio code, ISDN support, and other legacy network code. VME — a modular bus standard that dates back to the 1980s and found use in industrial, scientific, and embedded systems — is next in the queue.</p>
<p>The underlying dynamic is a shift in who has trouble, not who can write code. AI tools have sharply lowered the cost of authoring a patch: a contributor or an automated agent can produce plausible-looking changes to a dormant driver in seconds. For kernel maintainers, however, review capacity — not authoring capacity — has long been the bottleneck. Every patch against an unmaintained subsystem still consumes maintainer attention, whether it ends up being useful or not. Deleting dead code does not stop the flood of generated submissions, but it closes off one of the targets that attracts them.</p>
<p>That framing matters for anyone watching how open-source governance adapts to generative AI. The kernel community has historically been willing to accept trade-offs when a subsystem loses its maintainer: either someone forks the code, or vendors carry a downstream patch set. The VME code is effectively unused and obsolete, and Linux has generally treated that as sufficient grounds for removal rather than as a reason to preserve unmaintained code indefinitely.</p>
<p>There is a fair counterpoint worth keeping in view. VME hardware has not vanished entirely — variants remain in use in some niche industrial and defence settings, and those users typically rely on vendor-maintained kernels rather than mainline support. The removal, on that reading, is less a declaration that VME is extinct than an acknowledgement that mainline Linux was never the place its remaining users were getting support anyway.</p>
<p>In our own analysis, the practical implication for Hong Kong IT teams running long-lived enterprise Linux distributions is that the upstream kernel's appetite for retaining unmaintained legacy drivers will keep shrinking — so hardware refresh or vendor-support paths should be planned proactively, rather than assuming a driver's presence in an older kernel implies it will survive future major versions.</p>
<p>The episode also illustrates an emerging asymmetry that extends well beyond the kernel. AI lowers the cost of producing contributions everywhere, which means the burden shifts onto whoever must evaluate them. In volunteer-driven projects with finite reviewer attention, the practical response to an AI-driven spike in submissions is increasingly to reduce the surface area under review — by deleting code that no longer carries its maintenance weight.</p>
<p>Phoronix reports the VME removal is proceeding through the normal kernel mailing-list process. The wider question — how much more legacy code gets judged not by its users but by the noise it attracts — is likely to recur as generative tools are pointed at more of the tree.</p>
