Title: Backblaze Puts File Contents Off-Limits to Advertisers — What Backup Buyers Should Ask Next
Content:
<p>Cloud backup provider Backblaze has moved to draw a line under questions about its advertising practices, telling Ars Technica that it does not share users' backup and storage files — or the contents of those files — with advertisers. The clarification, reported by the publication, appears to address the sharpest edge of the controversy, while what the assurance does not appear to cover is the broader question of what customer information, short of the files themselves, does move through the ad-tech supply chain.</p>
<p>The distinction matters. "We don't share your files" is a considerably narrower assurance than "we don't share information about your files." Backblaze's line to Ars is that the payload is off-limits: backups, stored objects and the files inside them stay out of the ad-tech chain — leaving the remainder of customer information as the open question.</p>
<p>For anyone procuring backup services, that gap is where the due diligence belongs. A vendor can honour the worst-case promise absolutely — never exposing document contents, database dumps or archived mail — while still passing account-level signals, usage telemetry, or billing and contact details to third parties. The interesting question for a buyer is therefore not whether the vendor reads your data, but which metadata about your data, and about your account, is treated as shareable.</p>
<p>Metadata that can be recombined deserves particular scrutiny. Individually innocuous fields — file counts, backup frequency, storage volume trends, device types, geographic region — can be assembled into a surprisingly detailed picture of an organisation's size, activity patterns and infrastructure footprint. None of that requires opening a single backup archive. Whether a provider's advertiser relationships cover such telemetry, or only basic contact and billing records, is exactly what a prospective customer should pin down in writing before signing.</p>
<p>Hong Kong buyers evaluating backup vendors should frame that question against the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Seen through Hong Kong's PDPO, that gap maps onto purpose limitation under Data Protection Principle 1, which requires that personal data be collected for a purpose directly related to the function of the collector and used only for that stated purpose — a principle that sits awkwardly alongside open-ended advertising use of customer information.</p>
<p>Those obligations are framed around personal data rather than corporate account records, and enforcement is complaint-driven. But the standard they set is a useful yardstick against which contractual terms can be measured, regardless of where a backup provider is incorporated.</p>
<p>The practical takeaway for IT teams is a short checklist to run against any backup or storage proposal:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scope of sharing</strong> — Is the vendor's commitment limited to file contents, or does it explicitly cover account metadata and usage telemetry?</li>
<li><strong>Sub-processors</strong> — Which advertising, analytics or tracking third parties appear in the vendor's disclosed processor list?</li>
<li><strong>Contractual alignment</strong> — Do the service agreement's data-use clauses match the assurances given in marketing material, and are they enforceable?</li>
<li><strong>Retention</strong> — How long are operational logs and account records kept, and is that tied to a stated purpose?</li>
<li><strong>Jurisdiction and remedies</strong> — Where is data handled, and what recourse does the contract provide if a sharing practice falls outside expectations?</li>
</ul>
<p>Backblaze's assurance addresses the scenario organisations worry about most: a backup provider handing file contents to advertisers. But as with any vendor claim, the confirmatory step belongs in the contract, not the press response — the metadata practices the press response leaves unsettled are precisely the ones a written, scope-explicit data-use clause should settle before data moves.</p>
<hr>
<p>雲端備份服務供應商 Backblaze 已就其廣告做法的質疑劃下界線，向 Ars Technica 表示，公司並不會將用戶的備份及儲存檔案——以及這些檔案的內容——分享予廣告商。該報道稱，此澄清似乎回應了爭議中最尖銳的一環；然而，這項保證看來並未涵蓋較宏觀的問題：除了檔案本身之外，究竟還有什麼客戶資料會經過 ad-tech 供應鏈流轉。</p>
<p>這區別至關重要。「我們不會分享你的檔案」是一項遠較「我們不會分享與你檔案相關的資料」狹窄的保證。Backblaze 向 Ars 的說法是：payload 屬於禁區——備份、已儲存 object 及其中的檔案一律不進入 ad-tech 鏈——這反而令其餘客戶資料成為懸而未決的問題。</p>
<p>對任何採購備份服務的人士而言，這個缺口正是盡職審查（due diligence）應聚焦之處。供應商可以絕對兌現最壞情況的承諾——永不暴露文件內容、數據庫 dump 或封存電郵——卻同時把帳戶層面的訊號、使用 telemetry，或帳單及聯絡資料交予第三方。對買家而言，有趣的問題因此並非供應商會否讀取你的數據，而是你的數據及帳戶的哪些 metadata 被視為可以分享。</p>
<p>可重新組合的 metadata 尤須審視。個別看似無害的欄位——檔案數量、備份頻率、儲存量趨勢、裝置類型、地理區域——經組合後，卻可拼湊出一幅頗為詳盡的圖像，涵蓋機構規模、活動模式及基建足跡。以上種種均無須打開任何一個備份 archive。供應商的廣告商關係是否涵蓋這類 telemetry，抑或僅限基本的聯絡及帳單紀錄，正是潛在客戶在簽署協議前應以書面方式厘清的事項。</p>
<p>香港買家評估備份供應商時，應以此問題對照《個人資料（私隱）條例》。從香港的 PDPO 觀之，此缺口映現於《保障資料原則》第一原則下的目的限制——個人資料須為與收集者的職能直接相關的目的而收集，且只可用於該明示目的；此原則與對客戶資料作無明確界線的廣告用途並存時，顯得格格不入。</p>
<p>這些責任針對的是個人資料而非企業帳戶紀錄，且執行屬投訴主導模式。然而，它們所設立的標準是一把有用的尺度，可據以衡量合約條款，不論備份供應商在何地註冊成立。</p>
<p>對 IT 團隊而言，實質的結論是一份可套用於任何備份或儲存方案的簡短清單：</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>分享範圍</strong> — 供應商的承諾是否僅限於檔案內容，抑或明確涵蓋帳戶 metadata 及使用 telemetry？</li>
<li><strong>Sub-processors</strong> — 供應商披露的 processor 清單中，有哪些廣告、分析或追蹤類第三方出現？</li>
<li><strong>合約一致性</strong> — 服務協議中的數據使用條款，是否與市場推廣材料所作的保證一致，並且可強制執行？</li>
<li><strong>保留期限</strong> — 營運 log 及帳戶紀錄保存多久，是否與明示目的掛鈎？</li>
<li><strong>司法管轄及補救途徑</strong> — 數據在哪裏處理，若某項分享做法超出預期，合約提供什麼補救途徑？</li>
</ul>
<p>Backblaze 的保證針對的是機構最擔心的情境：備份供應商把檔案內容交予廣告商。然而，與任何供應商聲明一樣，確認步驟應落在合約之中，而非新聞回應——新聞回應未有厘清的 metadata 做法，恰恰是那份書寫清楚、範圍明確的數據使用條款在數據流動之前應當了結的事項。</p>
